Neither the word optimism nor pessimism appears in the Bible. Both are modern terms derived from Latin (optimus meaning "best" and pessimus meaning "worst") that entered everyday vocabulary centuries after the biblical texts were written and translated. However, Scripture extensively addresses both outlooks through concepts like hope, faith, trust, and joy versus despair, anxiety, cynicism, and a "downcast" spirit. Rather than framing them as fixed personality traits, the Bible treats them as spiritual postures, acknowledging that reality is often harsh while calling believers to ground their expectations in God's character rather than their immediate circumstances. No mobile-guide activity in this section. Numbers 13:27–28: the spies report that the land flows with milk and honey, but its inhabitants are strong and its cities fortified. Numbers 13:30–31: Caleb says, "Let us go up at once, and possess it; for we are well able to overcome it." The others respond, "We be not able to go up against the people; for they are stronger than we." Numbers 13:33: "We were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight." Their view of themselves was pessimistic and not grounded in what God had told them. They became discouraged and did not believe they were strong enough to enter and conquer the land. Numbers 14:8–9: Joshua and Caleb say, "If the LORD delight in us, then he will bring us into this land," and urge the people not to fear. They saw the same circumstances, but they understood that the LORD was with them. They looked beyond their circumstances to God's strength to overcome what stood before them.
